**Glossary**

**Agile / Scrum terms**

Sprint (iteration): A fixed time box, usually 1-2 weeks, in which the team commits to a set of work and aims to finish it. At the end you have something working.

Velocity: How much work your team actually finishes per sprint, measured in whatever unit you estimate in (story points, tasks, or days). If you completed 20, 24, and 22 points in your last three sprints, your velocity is about 22. You use it to predict: “We have 120 points of Must-haves left, at 22 per sprint that’s about 5.5 sprints.” Velocity is a planning tool, not a performance score. It’s only useful once you have 2-3 sprints of data, and it’s specific to your team.

Story points: A relative size estimate for a piece of work, usually on a scale like 1, 2, 3, 5, 8. They measure effort plus complexity plus uncertainty, not hours. If you’re solo or tiny, estimating in ideal days works fine too.

Retro (retrospective): A short meeting at the end of a sprint where the team asks: what went well, what didn’t, and what will we change next sprint? The output should be one or two concrete changes, not a long list. Solo, this is 15 minutes of writing it down.

Sprint review / playtest: Showing what was built to stakeholders or players and collecting feedback. In games it’s usually “play the current build.”

Backlog: The prioritized list of everything that might be done. Grooming / refinement is the ongoing work of clarifying, splitting, and estimating the top items.

Definition of Ready: The checklist an item must meet before entering a sprint (clear description, acceptance criteria, estimated, no blockers). Definition of Done: The checklist for calling it finished (works, tested, merged, no known bugs).

Spike: A time-boxed investigation to answer a question or reduce risk, such as “Can we get 60fps with 200 enemies? 2 days.” The output is knowledge, not a feature.

WIP (work in progress) limit: A cap on how many items can be in a column at once, so you finish things instead of starting everything.

MoSCoW: Must / Should / Could / Won’t priorities, useful when the deadline is fixed.

**Game development terms**

Phase / milestone: A stage of the project with a goal and a date. Typical order is concept → prototype → pre-production → production → alpha → beta → gold → post-launch. In my earlier schedule I used “phase” loosely to mean these checkpoints.

Prototype: A rough, quick build to test whether an idea is fun, often with placeholder art. It’s expected to be thrown away.

Core loop: The main thing the player repeats, like run → jump → collect → upgrade. If this isn’t fun, nothing else will save the game.

Vertical slice: One small, fully playable piece of the game that goes through all the layers at near-final quality: a bit of gameplay, art, sound, UI, and polish. For example, one complete level with the real player controller, a couple of enemies, menus, and audio. It proves the game works and shows what the finished game will feel like. Compare that to a horizontal slice, where you build one layer across the whole game (all the code, then all the art), which proves little until the end.

Greybox / blockout: Building levels or features with plain placeholder shapes to test layout and gameplay before art exists.

Alpha: The game is feature complete (everything is in, even if rough, buggy, or with placeholder content). Testing and balancing begin.

Feature freeze: The point after which no new features are added. Only bug fixes and polish. It protects the deadline.

Content complete: All levels, assets, and text exist in at least final-ish form.

Beta: Content is complete too. The focus is only bug fixing, performance, balance, and polish. Often shared with outside testers.

Release candidate (RC): A build you believe could ship. If testing finds no serious bugs, it becomes the final build. If it does, you fix them and make a new RC.

Gold (going gold): The final, approved build that gets released or submitted. The term comes from “gold master,” the disc sent to manufacturing. Today it just means “this is the version we’re shipping.” Often a day-one patch follows for last-minute fixes.

Certification / submission: For platforms like consoles, the App Store, or Steam, you submit the build for review. This can take days or weeks and can reject you, which is why I suggested treating your real deadline as earlier than the end of March.

Polish / juice: The small touches that make a game feel good, like screen shake, particles, sound effects, and animation easing. It’s easy to underestimate how long this takes.

Crunch: Working excessive hours to hit a deadline. Good planning and scope cuts are how you avoid it.

Post-launch / live ops: Patches, updates, and DLC after release. Ideas that miss the feature freeze go on this list.
